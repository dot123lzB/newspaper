The Weight of 1,138 Votes
The Day's Column · ★★★☆ C1

We live in an age of instant answers. A score appears before the whistle blows. Yet democracy still moves at the speed of paper.

This morning, a Senate seat in Mississippi depends on 1,138 votes, about one in every 800 ballots cast for the two leading candidates. In Alaska the gap is 637. Somewhere in those states, officials are opening envelopes, checking signatures and counting again.

It is easy to call this slow. It is more accurate to call it careful: each vote belongs to a person who trusted it would be counted, and the wait is the price of that promise. A thin margin will disappoint one side whoever wins, but the machinery of counting deserves our patience as much as our scrutiny.

The markets will move in milliseconds today. The count in Mississippi will move in envelopes. Both will give us an answer. Only one asks us to wait for it.

Democrats Win Both Houses of Congress in Stinging Rebuke of Trump

★★★☆ C1U.S. MIDTERM ELECTIONS

WASHINGTON — Democrats won control of the House of Representatives and the Senate in Tuesday's midterm elections, according to projections by the Associated Press and every major U.S. television network. It is a major setback for President Donald Trump, with two years left in his term.

The House was called first, just before 10 p.m. Eastern time, and the Senate about ninety minutes later. By early Thursday the AP counted 240 Democratic House seats to 147 Republican, with 48 races still open. Networks project a final Democratic total of roughly 250 to 268, which NBC says would be the largest House majority in nearly two decades.

In the Senate, Democrats needed a net gain of four seats and have so far picked up seven. In Texas, James Talarico defeated Ken Paxton, the first Democratic Senate win there in 38 years, according to CNN. Troy Jackson unseated Susan Collins in Maine, 53 percent to 47, and Sherrod Brown beat Jon Husted in Ohio. Democrats also flipped the governorships of Georgia and Texas.

Exit polls pointed to the cost of living and the war with Iran, which began on Feb. 28 and pushed fuel prices to records. Early exit polls put the president's approval at 38 percent. Hakeem Jeffries, running for Speaker, promised quick votes to restore health-insurance subsidies and end the tariffs; Speaker Mike Johnson said Republicans “will not be deterred by the outcome of one election.” Mr. Trump keeps his veto, and Senate Republicans keep the filibuster.

Oil Falls as Vance Blames Iran War for Defeat; Qatar Floats a Deal

Brent drops to a two-month low, but tankers are still being hit near the Strait of Hormuz.
★★★☆ C1ENERGY & THE GULF

Vice President JD Vance wrote on X that the war with Iran was why Republicans lost. Mr. Trump called that “largely true,” but said he had no regrets and told the Financial Times he had no immediate plans to strike Iran. Traders took it as a sign the war may be winding down. Brent crude fell about 8 percent on Wednesday and traded near $93.6 on Thursday, a two-month low and roughly a fifth below last week's peak of $116–118. U.S. crude settled at $88.12.

Qatari mediators are seeking American and Iranian backing for a draft agreement that could restart direct talks within days, through three days of confidence-building steps. Tehran demands an end to the U.S. naval blockade, sanctions relief and the release of frozen funds before it reopens the Strait of Hormuz. Neither government has confirmed the draft, so it should be read as a possibility, not a deal.

The danger has not passed. Iran's Revolutionary Guards said two tankers and a tugboat caught fire in the strait, and one tanker hit a mine. Another tanker was struck by two projectiles north of Qatar, according to the UKMTO. In Washington, CBS reports Mr. Trump has asked aides whether Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth should stay; the White House says he has “full confidence” in him.

Tokyo Calls the U.S. Alliance Its ‘Cornerstone’ as Officials Fear ‘Legacy Deals’

Japan stresses continuity after the U.S. vote, while worrying privately that a weakened president may court Beijing and Pyongyang.

TOKYO — The Japan–U.S. alliance is the cornerstone of Japan's foreign and security policy whatever the election result, Chief Cabinet Secretary Minoru Kihara said on Wednesday. Dow Jumps 1,569 Points on ‘Election Clarity,’ but AI Stocks Fall

Investors cheer divided government and cheaper oil, but fret over possible curbs on data centers.
★★★☆ C1WALL STREET · NOV 4 SESSION

NEW YORK — The Dow Jones Industrial Average jumped 1,569.40 points, or 3.03 percent, to 53,356.12 on Wednesday, its best day since April 2025. The S&P 500 rose 1.58 percent to 7,688.62, and the Nasdaq Composite gained 1.44 percent to 25,736.51. CNN called it the Dow's best post-midterm day in 44 years.

Investors read gridlock in Washington as a brake on big spending, and the sharp fall in oil eased inflation fears. Defense stocks such as Northrop Grumman, RTX and Lockheed Martin fell, because Mr. Trump's $1.5 trillion defense budget request now faces a tougher Congress.

The builders of artificial-intelligence infrastructure did not join the party. Nvidia fell 3 percent, and an ETF tracking AI-infrastructure stocks lost 9 percent, according to CNN. Several newly elected Democratic governors campaigned on restricting data centers (see our Deep Dive, page 7). The dollar index slipped to 101.41 in Asian trade on Thursday.

The Data-Center Backlash: How AI's Hunger for Power Became a Campaign Issue

Markets sold AI infrastructure after the midterms. The cause is not only Washington politics: from New York to Texas, voters are asking who pays for the electricity.
★★★☆ C1DEEP DIVE · POLITICS, ENERGY, MARKETS

Governor races with pause-backing candidates (pre-election analysis)

The market message. When the results came in, the clearest losers on Wall Street were the companies that build the machinery of artificial intelligence. An exchange-traded fund tracking AI-infrastructure stocks fell 9 percent on Wednesday and Nvidia fell 3 percent, while the Dow rose more than 3 percent. Analysts kept giving the same reason: fear of tighter rules on data centers. Goldman Sachs warned that Democratic statehouse wins could put roughly 45 percent of planned U.S. data-center capacity at risk, and a CNBC voter poll found 56 percent of voters called data centers a crucial or major factor.

Why voters care. The complaint is usually less about AI than about the household bill: data centers use huge amounts of electricity, and opponents argue that ordinary customers end up paying for the power plants and power lines built to serve them. Residential electricity prices were forecast to rise about 4 percent in 2026, after roughly 5 percent in 2025, according to CNBC. Higher utility bills were also a key factor in Democrat Abigail Spanberger's landslide win in Virginia's 2025 governor's race.

It is not only a Democratic cause. Bernie Sanders has called for a national moratorium, but Florida's Republican governor, Ron DeSantis, backs an “AI bill of rights” letting communities block data centers. In Michigan, both leading candidates for governor backed a pause: Democrat Jocelyn Benson proposed one year, and Republican John James proposed a halt until there is “a plan to protect the communities.” In Ohio, Republican Vivek Ramaswamy pledged to halt new approvals by executive order, while Democrat Amy Acton backed a conditional moratorium. In Texas, Gov. Greg Abbott announced a temporary pause, which Democrat Gina Hinojosa called “a scam” and said should be an immediate halt. Ms. Hinojosa, Ms. Acton and Georgia's Keisha Lance Bottoms, who wants construction stopped, were all elected on Tuesday, according to Fortune.

The arguments, side by side
For a pauseAgainst a pause
Sanders and Ocasio-Cortez: data-center growth burdens communities and makes residents subsidize Big Tech's energy use; a freeze should last until laws ensure new centers do not raise consumer electricity rates or worsen climate change.

Gov. Hochul: pause large projects while the state studies grid, water, air and climate effects; make developers help fund grid upgrades and reconsider tax incentives.		 White House AI adviser David Sacks: a national freeze would let China win the AI race. Interior Secretary Doug Burgum and Sen. John Fetterman, a Democrat, called a pause a “surrender flag” to Beijing.

The administration prefers market solutions, such as a voluntary “Ratepayer Protection Pledge” in which big tech firms absorb more of their energy costs. Georgia's Republican Gov. Brian Kemp warned that Ms. Bottoms's approach means “moratoriums on growth.”

What this means for markets

Wednesday's 9 percent fall in one AI-infrastructure fund prices a fear, not a law. Goldman's 45 percent is an estimate of capacity at risk, not capacity cancelled. So far only one state, New York, has an order in force, and most restrictions have come from counties and towns. The federal bill, which faced long odds in the old Congress, still needs Mr. Trump's signature, and he keeps his veto.

The risk for investors is therefore less a single national ban than a patchwork: different rules, delays and costs from state to state. Japan's market is exposed because chip and memory makers such as Kioxia, Advantest and Tokyo Electron sell into the AI build-out. GCI Asset Management's Takamasa Ikeda said the Democratic wins “fuelled caution for stricter regulations on AI infrastructure investment.”

The Data Center Watch newsletter adds a caution of its own: candidates in several states have linked election losses to controversial data-center projects, but whether those projects were decisive is unclear.

Editor's assessment — what the evidence supports, and what it does not

1. The driver is the power bill. The common thread across parties is electricity prices and grid strain, not hostility to AI itself. That is why Republicans and Democrats alike have proposed pauses.

2. Cost-sharing looks likelier than bans. The measures that have actually passed or been proposed mix pauses with rules on who pays: New York wants developers to fund grid upgrades, Maine ended tax breaks, and the administration offers a voluntary pledge. In my reading, the most probable near-term result is rules about who pays, not a blanket freeze. This is a judgment, not a certainty.

3. The vote may be a trigger, not the cause. The election gave investors a concrete political risk to price. Whether the fear lasts depends on what new governors actually sign.

4. Things to watch: the first executive orders from newly elected governors; the results of New York's study; utility rate cases; whether a divided government takes up ratepayer protections; and how the biggest AI companies describe their spending plans. This is analysis, not investment advice.
